Egyptian Nobel prize winners
  • Naguib Mahfouz (writer)
  • Ahmed Zewail (scientist)
  • Anwar Sadat (President of Egypt)

Naguib Mahfouz (writer)

Umm Kulthoum Mahfouz and Fatma Naguib Mahfouz receiving the Nobel Prize in literature 1988 on behalf of their father Naguib Mahfouz at the Nobel Prize award ceremony in Stockholm, Sweden on 10 December 1988. Nobel Foundation.Photo: Lars Åström


  • Born on 11 December 1911 in the old Gamaliya district of Cairo. He died in Cairo on 30 August 2006.
  • He studied philosophy at Cairo University and worked as a government employee for most of his life while writing.
  • He wrote more than 30 novels and hundreds of short stories. Many of them are set in the old neighborhoods of Cairo.
  • His most famous work is the Cairo Trilogy (Palace Walk, Palace of Desire, Sugar Street), which follows three generations of a Cairo family.
  • In 1988 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature. He was the first Arabic-language writer to receive it.
  • His novel Children of the Alley caused controversy and was banned in Egypt for many years.
  • In 1994 he survived an assassination attempt. Afterward, he could write for only a few minutes a day.

Ahmed Zewail (scientist)

Ahmed Zewail receiving his Nobel Prize from His Majesty the King at the Stockholm Concert Hall 1999. Copyright © The Nobel Foundation 1999 Photo: Hans Mehlin Ahmed Zewail receiving his Nobel Prize from His Majesty the King at the Stockholm Concert Hall 1999.


  • Born on 26 February 1946 in Damanhur and grew up in Desouk, in the Nile Delta. He died on 2 August 2016 in the United States and was buried in Egypt.
  • He studied at Alexandria University, then earned his PhD at the University of Pennsylvania.
  • He became a professor at Caltech (California Institute of Technology).
  • He is called the "father of femtochemistry." He used ultra-fast lasers to watch atoms move during chemical reactions, on a scale of femtoseconds (a millionth of a billionth of a second).
  • In 1999 he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry alone. He was the first Egyptian and first Arab to win a science Nobel.
  • He served as a US science envoy to the Middle East and founded the Zewail City of Science and Technology in Cairo.

Anwar Sadat (President of Egypt)

From left to right: Menachem Begin, Jimmy Carter and Anwar Sadat in Camp David, 7 September 1978. Photo: Bill Fitz-Patrick. Source: Jimmy Carter Library. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.


  • Born on 25 December 1918 in the village of Mit Abu al-Kum, in the Monufia governorate of the Nile Delta.
  • He graduated from the Royal Military Academy in Cairo in 1938.
  • He was a member of the Free Officers, the group that overthrew King Farouk in the 1952 revolution. Sadat was the one who announced the revolution on the radio.
  • He served as Vice President under Gamal Abdel Nasser and became President after Nasser's death in 1970.
  • He led Egypt in the October War of 1973, when the Egyptian army crossed the Suez Canal. The anniversary, 6 October, is still a national holiday in Egypt.
  • In 1977 he became the first Arab leader to make an official visit to Israel, where he gave a speech to the Israeli parliament (the Knesset).
  • In 1978 he signed the Camp David Accords with Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin, with US President Jimmy Carter as mediator.
  • That same year he shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Begin, becoming the first Egyptian to win a Nobel Prize.
  • In 1979 Egypt and Israel signed a peace treaty, and Egypt later recovered the Sinai Peninsula.
  • His peace policy was controversial in the Arab world, and Egypt was suspended from the Arab League for about ten years.
  • He also introduced the Infitah ("opening"), an economic policy that opened Egypt to private and foreign investment.
  • He was assassinated on 6 October 1981 during a military parade celebrating the anniversary of the October War. He is buried at the Unknown Soldier Memorial in Cairo.
  • His autobiography is titled In Search of Identity (1978).
