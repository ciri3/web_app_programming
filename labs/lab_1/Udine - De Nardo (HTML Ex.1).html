Loggia del Lionello

History

The construction of the Loggia del Lionello was proposed in 1441 by noble deputy Nicolò Savorgnan to provide Udine with a worthy municipal palace. Funded by local bread duties, building began in 1448 under the supervision of Nicolò Lionello and master builder Bartolomeo delle Cisterne. After debates regarding access stairs and layout, Lionello designed an exterior staircase solution. The city council held its first meeting there in 1455, and the project was completed around Lionello's death in 1462.

Architectural Style

Located in Piazza Libertà, the building exemplifies Venetian Gothic civic architecture. Its exterior is characterized by alternating horizontal bands of white and pink stone. The ground floor opens to the public space with a ten-arch portico reached via a broad central staircase. The upper level features side three-light mullioned windows and a central five-light window flanked by ornate stone emblems and the Gothic inscription UTINUM, the Latin name of the city, facing the castle hill.

Restorations and Art

Over the centuries, the loggia endured significant damage, notably from the 1511 Friuli earthquake and a devastating fire in 1876. Restorations included an internal open staircase designed by Giovanni da Udine in the sixteenth century and comprehensive nineteenth-century works overseen by Andrea Scala. The building preserves notable artworks, including a corner Madonna and Child by Bartolomeo Bon, an allegorical statue of the Patria del Friuli by Andrea Flaibani, and a nineteenth-century fresco by Giuseppe Ghedina.

Fun Fact: Electric Udine

Inventor Arturo Malignani, who designed the weather instruments beneath the loggia, made Udine one of the first European cities with widespread public electric street lighting in the late 19th century, well before patenting key incandescent bulb methods bought by Edison.

Piazza San Giacomo

Historical Role

Known officially as Piazza Giacomo Matteotti, the square has historically been called Piazza San Giacomo, Mercatonuovo, or Piazza delle Erbe. Established in the thirteenth century as a commercial hub to expand beyond the older Mercatovecchio, it developed into Udine’s daily gathering place. Surrounding porticoes hosted market vendors, guilds, and social gatherings, cementing the square's long-standing status as the lively civic and social center of the city.

Church of San Giacomo

The western edge of the square is dominated by the Church of San Giacomo Apostolo, originally founded in 1378 and rebuilt in 1525 with a Renaissance facade by Bernardino da Morcote. The front features a public clock and a bell gable, accompanied by an elevated churchyard terrace. At its center stands a sixteenth-century Marian column, flanked nearby by the polygonal well of 1486 and the monumental stone fountain designed in 1543 by Giovanni da Udine.

Architecture

The perimeter of the square forms a cohesive Renaissance and medieval urban ensemble framed by continuous ground-floor porticoes. Many multi-story residential buildings preserve remnants of decorative external frescoes featuring geometric patterns and soft historic pastel tones. Today, the square remains entirely pedestrianized, filled with open-air café seating that perpetuates its traditional role as Udine’s prime public living room and venue for events.

Curiosity: The Friulian Tajut Tradition

The outdoor café culture thriving under Piazza San Giacomo's arcades is the historic home of the tajut, the traditional ritual of gathering for a modest drop of local Friulano or Refosco wine, originally sipped standing up between market trades.

Porta Manin

Medieval Origins

Porta Manin, originally known as Porta San Bartolomeo, was erected in the late thirteenth century as part of the fourth circle of medieval defensive walls of Udine. Positioned along a key access axis connecting the historic center to the eastern territories, it served both as a fortified city gateway and a customs checkpoint. It draws its current name from the prominent Venetian noble Manin family, whose historic residence and ancestral estates developed in its immediate vicinity.

Structure

Originally a tall defensive tower, the structure underwent significant modifications between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as medieval fortifications lost their military utility. The upper watchtower elements were lowered and adapted into residential and administrative spaces. Today, the gate presents a sturdy vaulted passageway beneath a masonry arch, flanked by adjoining buildings that integrate the former defensive gatehouse seamlessly into the evolving urban streetscape.

Modern Urban Context

Unlike several other gates of Udine's outer defensive rings that were demolished during nineteenth-century urban modernization, Porta Manin survived relatively intact. Today, the arched opening provides a pedestrian link between Via Manin and the surrounding historic quarters, leading directly toward Piazza I Maggio and the Castle hill. It remains an evocative architectural marker that visually traces the historical expansion of medieval Udine’s perimeter.

Footnote: The Tiepolo Ceiling Mystery

Just steps away, the adjacent Palazzo Manin once held a grand ceiling fresco by Giambattista Tiepolo. Sold off by heirs during financial downturns in the late 19th century, the masterpiece passed through several private collectors before landing in London.
